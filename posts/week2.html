The question

Marvel is full of hubs. Spider-Man and a handful of other characters touch enormous parts of the network, and hubs alone can create patterns that look meaningful. So instead of asking whether Marvel differs from a completely random graph, we asked a stricter question.

If every character keeps exactly the same number of neighbors, but we scramble who those neighbors are, does Marvel still look special?

What we did

We loaded the same frozen Week 1 Marvel snapshot used by the course, collapsed directed Wikipedia links to undirected pairs, and extracted the 277-character giant component. We measured the real average local clustering coefficient, then built 100 null networks by repeatedly applying degree-preserving double-edge swaps; every character therefore keeps exactly the same degree in every null network.

The page computes the null distribution in your browser. It reports the null mean and standard deviation, the real network's z-score, and the empirical p-value. We also accumulate each character's local clustering across the null ensemble to identify neighborhoods whose triangle closure is unusually high even after controlling for degree.

What this does not prove: excess clustering does not tell us the social or editorial mechanism that created the triangles. It only tells us that the degree sequence by itself is not enough.
